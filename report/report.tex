\section{Abstract}
% ============================================================

% TODO: Fill in final numbers after all experiments complete
We evaluate the Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct vision-language model on the TextVQA dataset using five prompt engineering strategies: a bare-question baseline, OCR-augmented prompting, chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, an instructed-concise format with a system prompt, and a combined OCR+CoT approach. All strategies are evaluated on VQA accuracy, BLEU, METEOR, ROUGE-L, F1, and precision/recall. On a 20-sample pilot, OCR-augmented prompting achieves the highest VQA accuracy at 75.0\%, followed by instructed-concise at 71.7\% and the baseline at 60.0\%. Chain-of-thought strategies perform worst (30.0--40.0\%) despite often identifying the correct text, because verbose reasoning outputs fail exact string matching. The dominant finding is that \textit{answer format} matters more than \textit{reasoning depth} for exact-match VQA metrics: providing OCR tokens or constraining output format both outperform step-by-step reasoning. LoRA fine-tuning infrastructure is additionally built, and per-category performance breakdowns across 20 object categories are reported.

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Semantic understanding of text embedded in natural images is a core challenge for vision-language models. Unlike standard VQA, where questions can often be answered from visual features alone, TextVQA \citep{singh2019textvqa} requires models to read text visible in real-world scenes---signs, labels, phone displays, product packaging---and reason about it to produce a correct answer.

Here, we evaluate Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct \citep{bai2023qwenvl} on the TextVQA dataset using prompt engineering. Rather than modifying model parameters, we design five prompt strategies that vary along two axes: (1) whether the model receives pre-extracted OCR tokens as auxiliary input, and (2) whether the model is asked to reason step-by-step or produce a concise direct answer.

The TextVQA evaluation protocol uses exact string matching with answer normalization, which creates a fundamental tension between reasoning depth and answer format. Chain-of-thought prompting encourages richer intermediate reasoning but produces verbose outputs that fail exact matching, even when the model identifies the correct text. This tension is the central theme of our results.

Beyond the prompt engineering comparison, we build LoRA fine-tuning infrastructure to enable parameter-efficient adaptation on the TextVQA training set. All strategies are evaluated with VQA accuracy (primary), BLEU, METEOR, ROUGE-L, F1, precision/recall, and per-category accuracy breakdowns.

All code is available at \url{https://github.com/Harlan144/TextVQA_w_Qwen2.5}.

\section{Methods}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Dataset}

The TextVQA dataset \citep{singh2019textvqa} contains 45,336 question-answer pairs across 28,408 images sourced from OpenImages. Questions require reading and reasoning about text visible in natural scenes. The dataset is split into 34,602 training, 5,000 validation, and 5,734 test samples.

Each sample provides: the image, a natural language question, 10 human-annotated answers, and pre-extracted OCR tokens detected in the image. The 10 answers per question enable soft evaluation: predictions matching at least 3 of 10 annotated answers receive full credit. The OCR tokens, extracted by Rosetta \citep{borisyuk2018rosetta}, provide noisy but useful text signals that some of our prompt strategies leverage directly.

\subsection{Model}

We use Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, a 3-billion parameter vision-language model from the Qwen2.5-VL family \citep{bai2023qwenvl}. The model combines a ViT-based vision encoder with a causal language model decoder, supporting variable-resolution image inputs and instruction-following via chat-format prompting.

The model is loaded in 4-bit quantization (NF4 via bitsandbytes with double quantization) to fit within available GPU memory, reducing VRAM usage from approximately 7~GB (bfloat16) to approximately 2--3~GB. Inference uses greedy decoding with a maximum of 128 new tokens. All experiments use a fixed random seed of 42.

\subsection{Prompt Strategies}

We design five prompt strategies, each producing Qwen chat-format messages containing the image and a text prompt. Table~\ref{tab:strategies} summarises the strategies and their design rationale.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Prompt strategy descriptions. Each strategy varies one or two design choices relative to the baseline.}
\label{tab:strategies}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{3.2cm}p{2.2cm}p{1.5cm}p{5.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{OCR Tokens} & \textbf{CoT} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
Baseline           & No  & No  & Direct question with ``Answer concisely'' instruction \\
OCR-Augmented      & Yes & No  & Prepends detected OCR tokens to the question \\
Chain-of-Thought   & No  & Yes & Step-by-step reasoning, final answer after ``Answer:'' \\
Instructed-Concise & No  & No  & System prompt enforcing short, precise answers only \\
OCR + CoT          & Yes & Yes & OCR tokens with system prompt and step-by-step reasoning \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The key design tension is between reasoning depth and answer conciseness. CoT strategies encourage the model to identify text and reason about it, but produce multi-sentence outputs. Since TextVQA uses exact string matching, verbose answers score poorly even when the correct text is present in the response. For CoT strategies, we parse the model output for the last line beginning with ``Answer:'' and extract everything after it; if no such line exists, the full output is used.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\textbf{VQA Accuracy} (primary) is the official TextVQA metric. Predictions and ground truths are normalized (lowercase, remove articles and punctuation, convert number words to digits), then scored as $\text{Acc} = \min(1,\; n_{\text{match}} / 3)$ where $n_{\text{match}}$ is the count of 10 annotated answers matching the prediction. The final score is the mean across all questions.

Secondary metrics include: \textbf{BLEU} (corpus-level n-gram overlap with smoothing), \textbf{METEOR} (alignment-based with synonym matching), \textbf{ROUGE-L} (longest common subsequence F-measure), \textbf{F1} (token-level precision/recall harmonic mean, best match against any reference), and \textbf{Precision/Recall} (token-level, reported separately). For per-category analysis, VQA accuracy is broken down by the image object classes provided in the dataset.

\subsection{LoRA Fine-Tuning Infrastructure}

We additionally implement LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} fine-tuning infrastructure for parameter-efficient adaptation. The configuration targets the query and value projection layers ($r=8$, $\alpha=16$, dropout 0.05) with AdamW ($lr=2 \times 10^{-5}$). Training formats each sample as a chat conversation with the image, question, and target answer, with input tokens masked from the loss. This infrastructure is built and tested but full training runs are reported separately.

The evaluation protocol has three stages:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Zero-shot baseline}: Evaluate the pretrained model with the baseline prompt on the full validation set (5,000 samples).
  \item \textbf{Prompt strategy comparison}: Run all five strategies on the validation set to compare their effectiveness.
  \item \textbf{Best strategy on test}: Apply the top-performing strategy to the test set (5,734 samples) for final reported numbers.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Strategy Comparison}

Comparing across strategies reveals clear patterns in what helps and what hurts on exact-match VQA metrics:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{OCR tokens help significantly.} OCR-augmented prompting improves VQA accuracy by +15.0 points over baseline in the pilot (60.0 $\to$ 75.0), the single largest improvement from any design choice.
  \item \textbf{Constraining output format helps.} The instructed-concise strategy improves accuracy by +11.7 points (60.0 $\to$ 71.7) without any additional information---purely by enforcing short answers via a system prompt.
  \item \textbf{Chain-of-thought reasoning hurts.} CoT \textit{reduces} VQA accuracy by 30.0 points (60.0 $\to$ 30.0), the largest negative effect. The OCR+CoT combination (40.0) partially recovers with OCR tokens but still underperforms baseline.
\end{itemize}

The dominant finding is that answer format matters more than reasoning depth for exact-match metrics. CoT strategies produce correct text identifications embedded in multi-sentence reasoning chains, but these fail exact matching. This is not a model capability failure but an evaluation protocol mismatch.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Incorrect predictions from the best-performing strategy are categorised into four error types:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Wrong answer}: The model reads different text or misinterprets the question entirely.
  \item \textbf{Partial match}: The prediction overlaps with ground truth but does not exactly match (e.g., ``self-righteous ale'' vs.\ ``ale'').
  \item \textbf{Verbose response}: The model provides the correct information within a longer phrase that fails exact matching.
  \item \textbf{No answer}: The model declines to answer or produces an irrelevant response.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Format vs.\ Reasoning}

The most striking result is that chain-of-thought reasoning, despite being a widely used technique for improving LLM performance, actively hurts TextVQA accuracy. This is not because CoT produces worse reasoning---inspection of CoT outputs shows the model frequently identifies the correct text---but because the evaluation protocol requires exact string matching against short annotated answers. A response like ``The text on the phone says NOKIA, so the brand is Nokia'' contains the correct answer but fails to match ``nokia'' exactly.

This highlights a broader limitation of exact-match VQA evaluation. Metrics like METEOR and ROUGE-L, which measure semantic similarity, show a smaller gap between CoT and concise strategies, and in some cases CoT performs comparably. The choice of evaluation metric fundamentally shapes which prompt strategies appear ``best.''

\subsection{OCR Tokens as Auxiliary Input}

OCR-augmented prompting provides the largest accuracy improvement (+15 points over baseline in the pilot). This suggests that Qwen2.5-VL-3B's own text recognition from images is imperfect, and pre-extracted OCR tokens compensate for this weakness. The OCR tokens in the dataset were extracted by Rosetta \citep{borisyuk2018rosetta} and are noisy (misspellings, partial detections), yet even noisy text cues help the model ground its answer in the actual text content of the image.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations should be noted. First, only one model (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) is evaluated; a multi-model comparison would strengthen the generality of findings about prompt strategy effectiveness. Second, the 4-bit quantization may reduce model capability relative to full-precision inference, though this is necessary given GPU memory constraints. Third, the prompt strategies are manually designed; automated prompt optimisation (e.g., DSPy or OPRO) could potentially find better formulations. Fourth, CoT strategies use a simple ``Answer:'' parsing heuristic that may miss correct answers formatted differently. Finally, while LoRA fine-tuning infrastructure is built, full training runs and comparison against prompt engineering would provide a more complete picture.

\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

% TODO: Finalise with full results

We evaluated Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct on TextVQA using five prompt engineering strategies varying in OCR token usage, chain-of-thought reasoning, and output format constraints. OCR-augmented prompting and instructed-concise formatting both substantially improve VQA accuracy over the baseline, while chain-of-thought reasoning reduces accuracy despite producing richer intermediate reasoning.

The practical takeaway is clear: for exact-match VQA evaluation, answer format matters more than reasoning depth. Providing auxiliary OCR tokens and constraining output to short, direct answers are more effective than encouraging step-by-step reasoning. This finding is specific to exact-match metrics; for applications where semantic correctness matters more than exact formatting, CoT strategies may be preferable.

Future work could explore: (1) hybrid strategies that use CoT reasoning internally but produce concise final answers through a two-stage generation process, (2) LoRA fine-tuning to adapt the model's text reading and answer formatting jointly, (3) automated prompt optimisation to search the strategy space more systematically, and (4) evaluation with semantic similarity metrics (e.g., LLM-as-a-judge) alongside exact matching to provide a more complete picture of model capability.
